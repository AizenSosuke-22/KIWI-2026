# Product Vision — Kivi

The majority of business in India happens through direct speech, not typing, especially in local and rural India, and even in most of urban India's smaller shops and businesses. But wherever an idea, a request, or a transaction needs to be translated into a written format, whether a legal filing, a formal application, an English-language document, or a prompt to an AI tool, language becomes a barrier. Ideas that could have moved forward stall, or never leave the head of the person who had them.

Kivi exists to close that gap. As a product of Sarvam, its job is to make sure that no idea in India remains just an idea because the person who had it could not write it down in the right language or the right format.

In the age of AI, this gap is widening, not shrinking. People use Claude, GPT, and similar tools to build and ship at speeds that were impossible a few years ago, but the entry ticket is still fluent written English. Millions of Indians who know exactly what they want to build or say are shut out for no reason other than the interface. Kivi should be the bridge that lets a spoken idea in Hindi, Tamil, or Hinglish become a finished application, a working prompt, or a formal document, without the person having to become a proficient English writer first.

That is why Kivi cannot be positioned as a dictation app. Dictation is what it does mechanically. What it *is* is a daily companion for anything you would otherwise have to write down: at work, in study, in personal life. In a world where everyone from students to corporate professionals is trying to boost productivity, research consistently shows that speaking is a faster and more natural way to get ideas out than typing. Kivi should be how Indians access that advantage. The measure of success is not that people use Kivi occasionally, but that a meaningful share of their daily written output ends up flowing through it.

The same capability has a second, larger destination: sectors where accurate spoken-to-written records are not a convenience but a legal and clinical necessity. Hospitals, courts, and government offices generate enormous volumes of spoken record-keeping every day, most of it still handled manually and imperfectly. As Sarvam scales, Kivi should evolve into an enterprise-grade offering for these sectors, replacing the clerical layer that currently slows Indian institutions and quietly produces avoidable errors. This is where Kivi's contribution becomes national rather than personal.

To make that vision real, three capabilities matter beyond what a good dictation tool already provides.

**Speak-to-formal-English translation.** A user should be able to speak in their own language and receive polished, professional written English suitable for a prompt, an application, or a document. Underlying models already support Indic languages, but the world still runs on written English, and this is the specific friction Indian users hit hardest.

**Language pattern insight.** Kivi should observe recurring patterns in a user's own speech and offer them a mirror, surfacing what they say often, where they might be unclear, and how their speech could sound more professional if they want it to. This is opt-in personalisation no competitor offers today.

**Personalisation done right.** Wispr Flow has set the bar for making a dictation tool feel individually owned. Kivi should meet or exceed that bar, drawing on the same principles, but never in a way that reads as an Indian copy of Wispr Flow. That is a positioning line the product must actively refuse to cross.
